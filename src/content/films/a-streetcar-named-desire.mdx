A Streetcar Named Desire is a film that I have a great deal of respect for and very little desire to revisit (pun intended); not out of a lack of quality, rather, due to the overbearing bleakness that haunts the narrative and the characters within it. A lot of the praise that I would like to give to the movie is credit to the original play by Tennessee Williams which is - in many ways - ahead of it's time, exploring a myriad of themes before they were widely explored in the cultural zeitgeist. The film, directed by Elia Kazan, is remarkably faithful to that vision, and is all the more suffocating for it.

The title itself is doing considerably more work than it might first appear. A streetcar named Desire was a real line that ran through New Orleans; although it doesn't make an appearance in the play, the film makes the decision to use it as the vehicle - quite literally - by which Blanche DuBois arrives at her sister's home. Desire is not merely a theme of the play; it is the engine by which every character is propelled, and often destroyed. Blanche is driven by a desire to recapture a past that no longer exists, Stanley by a desire for dominance and control, and Stella by a desire for stability that requires her to look away from things she cannot afford to see clearly. We see this clearly in the following exchange between Blanche and her sister Stella, when she is reprimanding him about Stanley:

  <Character

  <Character name="STELLA" line="Haven't you ever ridden on that streetcar?" />

    line="It brought me here. Where I'm not wanted and where I'm ashamed to be."

In this exchange, the streetcar acts as a literal proxy for desire itself, and is a metaphor for how her past has led her to her own current circumstances. The streetcar, and by extension desire itself, is not positioned as a vehicle of liberation but of arrival at precisely the wrong destination - a distinction that cuts to the heart of Blanche's situation in the movie. This is the first parallel that the movie draws between Blanche and Stanley - two characters who, on the surface, appear to be total opposites. Stanley is equally a creature of desire, and although Blanche isn't conscious of this parallel the narrative makes it evident that both characters are equally driven...

There are a myriad of themes underscoring the entire play that have already been analysed extensively. In the interest of not making this a full-blown analysis, I will leave these undiscussed, but a theme which particularly captured my attention was that of "illusions", and how these are dismantled as the movie progresses.

<Script> <Character name="BLANCHE" line="I know I fib a good deal. After all, a woman's charm is 50% illusion." /> </Script>

Blanche's journey to Stella and Stanley's house is allegorical in and of itself. She arrives at Elysian Fields - named for the Greek paradise of the afterlife - having travelled through a place called Cemeteries. The place names are real, but the route reads as a kind of fate: Blanche has passed through death to arrive at what should be sanctuary, and yet Elysian Fields, in practice, is anything but. She has reached the literal and metaphorical "end of the line" signalling the beginning of her downward mental spiral. The subsequent portrayal of Blanche is a sustained portrait of a woman who has chosen illusion as a survival strategy; Blanche exists almost exclusively in semi-darkness and shadow throughout the film, a detail aims to highlight how she has structured her entire existence around avoiding the harsh, clarifying glare of reality. Beyond simple affectations, the paper lanterns, the satin robes, and the carefully maintained performance of a Southern belle all point toward a deeper issue. If she can sustain the appearance, she can sustain the self-image, and if she can sustain the self-image, she does not have to reckon with the checkered past and destitute present that lie beneath it.

Vivien Leigh's portrayal of Blanche is almost theatrical in its nature - very poised, very deliberate, and deeply dramatic at times. This is, of course, an intentional choice rather than a limitation; Blanche is herself a performer, a woman who has spent so long constructing a version of herself that the performance has become inseparable from the person. Her performance especially stands out in the moments where her composure fractures and Blanche grows hectic and unravelling. Notwithstanding, her acting is still very theatrical in these moments. Comparatively, it feels like Marlon Brando exists in an entirely different film. Watching their scenes together is a genuinely surreal experience - it feels almost as though a contemporary actor has been dropped into a production from the 1950s. There are a myriad of nuances to his acting that feel extremely out of place amongst the backdrop of the more theatrical performances around him. Stanley's character is deeply unlikeable, which is a testament to Brando's performance as he does so while also being the most captivating presence in the movie.

Ultimately, the bleakness which haunts the movie is not incidental to the experience, but it is the experience itself. It's a film which I can admire for its masterful source material and the performances it draws out of its leading actors, while remaining perfectly content to never watch it again.
