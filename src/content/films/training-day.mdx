Following on from _500 Days of Summer yesterday_, this feels far closer to the flavour of movie that I would find myself typically watching. _Training Day_ follows green-faced rookie Jake Hoyt (Ethan Hawke) joining up with Alonzo Harris (Denzel Washington), a seasoned street-wise veteran, with the enthusiasm of a young burgeoning career-man excited at the prospect of using his experiences to progress further. However, Jake soon finds out that he is in for more than he bargained for as Alonzo's morality grows from being increasingly grey to outright criminal. This escalation is accompanied by a tension which grows from a lingering threat to something far more visceral and immediate. Alonzo frames every transgression as a necessary lesson, a rite of passage, the way things actually work out here. By the time the film's true nature reveals itself, Jake has already been implicated.

Denzel is widely regarded as one of the greatest performers that Hollywood has had to offer although, admittedly, I find myself unfamiliar with his filmography; my exposure to his work hitherto had been limited to Remember the Titans from the year prior to the release of _Training Day_ (which is a great movie in and of itself). His performance here rightfully deserved an Academy Award, distinguished by a commanding subtlety. The uneasiness that Hoyt feels is constantly felt by the audience as we are equally tense when faced with the volatility and sheer believability of Denzel's performance. Undoubtedly, the precise, calculated pressure he applies to Hoyt - along with the confident aggression with which he delivers each line - stands out as the highlight of the movie. 

To that extent, Ethan Hawke plays the role of an unassuming rookie with an equal fervour and naivety. Much like Denzel, my experience with his work has been equally limited. Sinister was shaping up to be one of my favourite horror movies of all time until the final act when the ghost kids began performing the long-jump from Super Mario 64 across tables, losing all sense of believability in the process. The final act of this movie serves Hawke's acting prowess considerably better as the final confrontation between Hoyt and Alonzo is accompanied with the deep satisfaction of seeing Hoyt's sense of justice prevail. In the face of corruption, deceit, and the risk of being incarcerated through association, Hoyt remains steadfast in his beliefs and refuses to be a piece on the proverbial chess board which Alonzo aims to be the grandmaster of.

Beyond the performances of the two lead actors, the setting the film finds itself in becomes almost a character in its own right. The Los Angeles of _Training Day_ is not the glamorous, sun-bleached city of Hollywood myth but something rawer and more labyrinthine. It's a world of competing loyalties, neighbourhood hierarchies, and unwritten rules that Jake has no map for. It's a movie which feels far more "street-level" than other movies of its type aim to be through its appreciation for the granular texture of the world it inhabits.

Ultimately, _Training Day_ was both exactly what I expected, while also being better than I expected.
