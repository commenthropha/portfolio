The King of Comedy almost felt like a movie of two halves. While the first half struggled to captivate me at times, the second half was a masterclass in directing and blurring the lines between reality and fiction. 

One of Scorsese's early works which I just had the chance to rewatch in cinemas as part of Cineworld's Scorsese season, The King of Comedy is a satirical black comedy which aims to deconstruct celebrity culture. Robert De Niro plays the role of Rupert Pupkin, an aspiring stand-up comedian still living with his mother who is infatuated with the idea of being a talk show host like his hero Jerry Langford (played by Jerry Lewis). After meeting Jerry, who promises Rupert he'll get back to him in an attempt to shrug him off, he becomes obsessive with the idea that his big break has finally arrived. Eventually, reaching a point left with no other option, he teams up with a fellow obsessive fan to kidnap Jerry in a desparate attempt to get onto TV.

Much of the first half is spent establishing the thinking behind Rupert Pupkin with a calculated patience. Although the movie is very much intended to be comedic at times, there is a lingering sadness to Pupkin's character - which, I suppose, is expected given the nature of a black comedy. Watching Robert De Niro play this delusioned superfan who refuses to take a hint is equal parts cringeworthy and almost tragic. Beneath his relentless self-belief is a deep desperation for recognition and validation. 

The second half is where I felt the movie really shines. The movie pulls the rug from under the audience by intentionally and carefully blurring the lines of what's actually happening compared to what exists in Rupert's imagination. What makes this especially effective is that these scenes are not elevated with a sense of grandiosity - rather, they are fantasies as mundane as a chat show appearance and meeting up with Rita in the bar to show her his television appearance. And, I suppose, these are what further provide these scenes with an air of melancholy. Although he refers to himself as the "king of comedy", all he imagines himself as is someone who's made it.

Speaking of Rita, when I watched the movie I felt that there was something profoundly heartbreaking about her character which transcended the movie's satirical nature. And I read a Letterboxd review which verbalised these very same sentiments. The way she tells Rupert "I guess you're entitled to come up for some coffee" is a really heartbreaking line once you think about it; despite clearly not enjoying their date, she feels as though she owes _him_ something because society has not provided her with a sense of self-worth beyond that. Throughout the entire movie, Rupert uses their dynamic solely as a way to receive further self-validation. Intentional or not, it was something which certainly stood out to me and I'm glad that other people picked up on it too.

Ultimately, the entire essence of Rupert's character - and the movie's criticism of celebrity culture - can be summarised with my favourite line from the movie.
