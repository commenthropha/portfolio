This marks the beginning of a series I'm going to pretentiously call 500 Days of Summer where, in an effort to watch more movies and begin writing about them, I'm going to watch a new movie every day for the next 500 days starting from this Summer. And, of course, what would be a more apt way to start than by watching _500 Days of Summer_.

I'm not someone who has a particular affinity for the romcom genre, but I did find this movie to be genuinely funny at times. A lot of that comes from the film's willingness to puncture its own sentimentality. The musical number through the park after Tom's first night with Summer is absurd in exactly the right way; it sets a trend for a lot of the humour in the movie going forward, whereby it exists in the uncomfortable gap between how Tom experiences things and how they actually are.

Tom Hansen (which feels way too familiar to the name of Johnny Depp's character in 21 Jump Street) is a man who believes in the idea of true love in the most architecturally pure sense - which is fitting, given that he's a greeting card writer who studied to be an architect architect. He doesn't just want a relationship, he wants _the_ relationship, the one that slots perfectly into the romantic narrative he's been building in his head since, the film implies, he first misheard The Smiths.

I don't think that Summer's actions in the movie are  morally dubious in any capacity, especially considering that she made her intentions clear from the absolute outset. Her simultaneous attachment to Tom, and also her lack thereof, was surmised at the very beginning of the movie, when we are introduced to Summer's outlook on love by stating she loved only two things: her long, dark hair, and how easily she could cut it off and feel nothing. This hair metaphor symbolises her emotional detachment, control, and ability to discard attachments without pain.

Ultimately, the two main characters represent the stark disparity between infatuation and reality. Tom is in love with a version of Summer that is largely his own construction - he curates her into a romantic ideal, ignoring the parts that don't fit the story he's telling himself. For example, the fact that she was never interested in a serious relationship. Summer, meanwhile, is simply living her life without any obligation to inhabit the role he's written for her. The tragedy isn't that she doesn't love him back in the way he wants, rather, it's that he spends the entire film treating that as a problem to be solved rather than a reality to be accepted.

The way the film handles the question of fate is also of interest. Tom is a true believer in soulmates and in the end, the film gives him exactly what he thinks he wants: he meets someone new, her name is Autumn, and the whole thing resets. It's played as a happy ending, and it sort of is, but it's also a little unsettling. Tom hasn't really grown so much as he's found a new object for the same projection. The film is too smart to fully commit to that reading, but it doesn't fully disavow it either, and that ambiguity is where it earns its keep.



What keeps the film from feeling like it's punishing Tom for being a romantic is that it's also honest about Summer's limitations. She's not a manic pixie dream girl, the film explicitly flags that reading and sidesteps it, but she's not a fully realised counter-argument to one, either. We understand her through her effect on Tom far more than we understand her through her own interiority, and you could argue that's the film's one structural concession to the very trope it's critiquing. It would've been interesting to have received a slightly deeper introspection, however I believe the little insight we receive at the beginning is sufficient for the narrative.

I think the tagline of the movie is a fairly apt descriptor of what to expect going into this film. 

<Quote>
    This is not a love story. This is a story about love.
</Quote>

Overall, a pretty fun watch and more enjoyable than I was expecting it to be.
