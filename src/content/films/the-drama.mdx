This is the first spoiler-free review that I'm writing, as I believe that a large part of the viewing experience for this movie is derived from the central plot point that drives it, and this is best experienced without any prior context. What I will say is that The Drama asks extremely bold questions about personal judgement and redeemability - the answers to which are left intentionally ambiguous and handed back to the viewer to sit with.

Robert Pattinson has well and truly completed his career renaissance since the days of Twilight. It's often very difficult for actors who are typecast to break out of it - an example that immediately comes to mind is Matthew McConaughey, who turned down many million-dollar contracts and returned to indie movies to find renewed credibility in grittier work. Pattinson's journey is very similar in that respect, having steadily rebuilt his reputation through lower-budget indie films such as Cosmopolis, The Rover, and - of course - Good Time. Notwithstanding, I would argue that Pattinson has a greater acting range than McConaughey, as illustrated by roles as varied as voicing the Heron in The Boy and the Heron. His role as Charlie in this movie is very much an extension of that range. There are myriad subtleties to his performance which don't go unnoticed: the way the veins on his forehead surface when he's stressed or tense, the way his eyes widen and contract in shock or fear. A large part of this nuance, I suspect, stems from his experience playing Batman - a role in which his entire performance was necessarily carried by his eyes within the cowl, and one which appears to have left him with a remarkably precise command of his facial expressions.

Equally, I believe that this is by far Zendaya's best acting role out of what I've seen from her so far. Admittedly, I'm yet to see Challengers, but even compared to Dune 2 - which I previously believed to be her best acting role - her portrayal of Emma is played with a nuance I haven't seen from her before. Where her previous work often asked her to project strength and composure, this film asks her to be genuinely unreadable at times, and she rises to that challenge with a confidence I haven't seen from her prior filmography. The audience's relationship with Emma mirrors Charlie's own - you're never entirely sure what to feel about her, and Zendaya is fully aware of that through her performance. Moreover, the two leads together also carry a believable chemistry; their relationship is established quickly but convincingly, and it needs to be - because once the film's central event occurs, the entire weight of what follows rests on whether you believe these two people were genuinely happy before it.

It's also worth noting how well the supporting cast serves the film. Alana Haim and Mamoudou Athie, as the married friends through whom much of the fallout is filtered. While it's widely agreed that Mike (Mamoudou Athie's character) was a true bro, Haim's character in particular is a source of divisiveness. At face value, she occupies the moral high ground, and the film initially frames her that way. But the more closely you watch her, the more the film seems to be asking whether she has entirely earned that position. There are details in her own behaviour, largely brushed past, that sit uncomfortably alongside the certainty with which she judges others. Her character functions as an effective parallel to the film's central moral question - the gap between intent and outcome, and how readily we extend grace to ourselves while withdrawing it from others. The fact that she shows little real self-awareness in this regard is precisely the point, with the movie appearing far more interested in exploring the audience's sympathies than in offering a clean moral hierarchy.

The Drama is a movie where the tension is almost suffocating at times, and this is not merely enabled but carried by the performances of the two lead actors and Borgli's multi-faceted writing. A strong entry in what is shaping up to be a phenomenal year for A24.
