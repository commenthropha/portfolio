Before I get into this one, I'm going to be slightly revisionist and change the rules of the challenge. In practice, I've realised that watching a _new_ film everyday has two significant flaws. Firstly, it would stop me from writing about films I'm experiencing for the first time in a cinema, thanks to Cineworld's reshowings of classic films — and those first-time theatrical experiences are some of the most exciting watches I've had doing this. Secondly, it would prevent me from revisiting films I genuinely want to write about, whether because I have a lot to say or because enough time has passed that I suspect I'd come at them from a completely different angle. So let's just pretend like these were the rules since the beginning.

Hamnet opens with the context that Hamlet and Hamnet were, in Elizabethan England, considered the same name. It's a quiet detail that reframes everything that follows and hangs over the entire film like a shadow; what Hamnet sets out to do is dramatise the life of William Shakespeare in the aftermath of losing his young son, and to ask, carefully and mournfully, how that grief might have found its way into one of the most celebrated plays ever written.

Jessie Buckley's performance as Agnes is the beating heart of this movie and her academy award for best actress was beyond well-deserved. Her portrayal of a grieving mother is one of the most viscerally believable performances I have seen in recent memory. Grief itself within this movie feels like a physical presence beyond an emotion, and Jessie Buckley inhabits it for large parts of the movie. And yet, she isn't the undisputed star of the film. That distinction belongs to Jacobi Jupe, playing Hamnet himself, whose performance is mind-blowing. There is a naturalism and an emotional intelligence to his performance that enables Hamnet to feel like a complete and vivid human being in the limited time the film affords him, which makes everything that follows land with the weight it needs to. On that note, the decision to then cast his real-life brother Noah Jupe as the actor playing Hamlet within the film's staging of the play is another way that the movie aims to highlight how Hamlet serves as a vicarious resurrection of Hamnet's hopes and dreams. 

The third act is where the film fully reveals the depth of its ambition; by the time we finally see the play, the film has built such a careful, unhurried architecture of grief that when it finally exhales, the emotional release feels entirely earned rather than artificially engineered. What struck me most about the movie as a whole, particularly when rewatching it, was how carefully the movie constructs its emotional register. It perpetually teeters on the edge of sadness without ever being overindulgent and reaching into obvious audience manipulation that a lesser movie would deploy without hesitation. On my first watch, I initially thought that my lack of shedding a tear was the movie "missing something". However, on reflection, I was wrong; I've come to understand that the movie's restraint is its greatest strength.

Hamnet is not interested in making you cry, nor is it building towards a cathartic release that it can engineer on your behalf. Instead, it lets the audience revel in whatever emotions the movie invokes in you, leading to a deeply personal viewing expereince. And, in the end, that is what separates great cinema from the kind that forces you to try and feel something.
