Kill Bill Vol. 1 is my favourite movie of all time; in myriad interviews for the movie, when asked about the casting of Chiaki Kuriyama for O-Ren Ishii's bodyguard - Gogo Yubari, Tarantino speaks about how he was so impressed with her performance in Battle Royale that he was adamant about casting her for the movie. Naturally, given the cult status of Battle Royale - alongside the macabre premise - this piqued my interest. Having garnered the praise of Tarantino, I was expecting something as stylistically bombastic if it was only that. Unfortunately, Battle Royale was neither fully committed to its premise nor did it lean into the B-movie-esque idiosyncrasies that are typical of Tarantino's own style. In the end, the movie almost came across as an acidentally black comedy and I'm still not sure what the movie was trying to be.

The premise, for the uninitiated, involves a class of Japanese students who are dumped on an island and forced to engage in a battle royale until only one person remains. Some may even say, a number one victory royale. However, what surprised me most about Battle Royale was not the premise, but how boring it manages to be despite the premise. A lot of the violence is gratuitous in the most underwhelming way possible. Notwithstanding, to the movie's credit, the landscape in which it was created looks very different to contemporary cinema. When Battle Royale was originally released, the premise alone was enough to garner widespead attention; the controversy surrounding it was a significant part of what cemented its cult status in the first place. However, as someone who is an avid lover of action movies, I found myself feeling that the movie never fully comitted to the premise it set out to achieve. It sits in a strange middleground where the violence is never visceral enough to be brutal nor is it over the top enough to feel as though the movie was embracing the absurdity of its premise. I can't tell if it's taking itself seriously at all.

When watching this movie, I couldn't help but compare it to Blue Spring, a Japanese youth drama which released the same year as Battle Royale. It is a far more personal movie, focussing on the violence and nihilism affecting a group of students as they worry about growing up and growing apart. The key impetus behind the movie's melanholic seriousness, despite the slightly elevated premise, is a fundamental understanding of the characters as complicated, multifaceted human beings with a myriad of complex emotions.

Even in spite of the bleakness, the movie also has an underlying beauty best illustrated by the scenes with the main character accompanying the groundskeeper learning to grow flowers until they flourish - serving a metaphor for the current situation that the movie's characters find themselves in, contrasting youthful cynicism with glorious purpose.

Which brings me to this movie's protagonist - Shuya Nanahara. He is, without question, one of the most generic, bland, uninteresting main characters that I have encountered in a film in a long time. If you think of any generic anime protagonist, this is your guy - typically cliche tragic backstory and all. The writing does him absolutely no favours either. The dialogue constantly veers between overwrought melodrama and the kind of earnest corniness that is the hallmark of a mid-90s shounen anime, the kind where the protagonist defeats the villain through power of friendship, accompanied by a swelling orchestral score and tears streaming down everyone's faces. There is a place for that kind of storytelling - that place is not a film that is simultaneously asking you to take its premise seriously.

In spite of all this, I will actually watch the sequel for this movie; it looks fun in the same way that Fred 2 was a fun watch after watching the first Fred movie. Although, to Fred 2's credit, John Cena playing Fred's dad should've at least got an academy award nomination.
